Anh Le-Duy Pham, Ph.D.

Welcome

My name is Anh Le-Duy Pham. I am an ocean biogeochemistry modeler interested in understanding the mechanisms that regulate ocean biogeochemical cycles, marine ecosystem dynamics, and their responses to environmental and climate change.

My research focuses primarily on the ocean iron (Fe) cycle and its connections to marine ecosystems and the carbon cycle. I combine numerical modeling, observational datasets, and large-scale model intercomparison to investigate how physical circulation, chemical processes, and biological interactions regulate the distribution and fate of iron in the ocean.

I received my Bachelor's degree in Marine Engineering from Hanoi University of Science in 2013 and my Ph.D. in Oceanography from the School of Earth and Atmospheric Sciences at the Georgia Institute of Technology in 2019.

I am currently a researcher in the Department of Atmospheric and Oceanic Sciences at the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA), where I work with Daniele Bianchi and collaborators on ocean biogeochemistry and ecosystem modeling.

Before joining UCLA, I was a postdoctoral researcher at LOCEAN and ISPL at Sorbonne University in Paris, France.

Research Highlights

Iron cycling along continental margins
I investigate how benthic, riverine, atmospheric, and biological sources of iron interact with scavenging, precipitation, ligand stabilization, and physical circulation to control the transport of iron from continental margins to the open ocean.
California Current System and the U.S. West Coast
My current regional research focuses on the California Current System, including the distribution and cycling of dissolved iron and organic ligands, shelf-to-basin transport, and the interaction between small-scale ocean circulation and biogeochemical processes.
Iron and marine ecosystems
I am extending ocean iron-cycle modeling toward marine food webs, including the effects of iron availability on phytoplankton, heterotrophic bacteria, zooplankton, and higher trophic levels such as fish.
Global ocean iron models and observations
I am evaluating dissolved iron distributions in CMIP6 and other ocean biogeochemical models against the GEOTRACES observational compilation, with the goal of identifying strengths and limitations of current representations of the marine iron cycle.
Carbon cycle and engineered marine ecosystems
I am also developing biogeochemical modeling approaches to investigate the transport and ecosystem impacts of polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB) released by engineered Sargassum, including potential consequences for marine food webs and the ocean carbon cycle.

Research

During my Ph.D., I used ocean biogeochemical models to investigate the mechanisms controlling the distribution and cycling of dissolved iron across the global ocean. My work examined the roles of biological uptake, organic ligand binding, scavenging, remineralization, and external iron sources.

During my postdoctoral research at LOCEAN and Sorbonne University, I expanded this work to investigate interactions between iron and marine organisms, particularly heterotrophic bacteria and zooplankton. This work explored how biological iron demand can influence iron recycling and broader ocean biogeochemical cycles.

At UCLA, my research focuses increasingly on the California Current System and the broader eastern Pacific Ocean. I combine high-resolution ocean circulation and biogeochemical modeling with observations to understand how mesoscale and submesoscale circulation influences iron transport and ecosystem dynamics.

Current research directions include:

  • Iron transport from continental margins to the open ocean
  • Iron and organic ligand cycling in the California Current System
  • Iron limitation of phytoplankton and heterotrophic bacteria
  • Iron recycling by zooplankton and implications for marine food webs
  • Iron controls on fish and higher trophic levels
  • Evaluation of CMIP6 ocean iron models using GEOTRACES observations
  • High-resolution ROMS-MARBL modeling of the California Current System
  • Biogeochemical consequences of engineered Sargassum and PHB production
